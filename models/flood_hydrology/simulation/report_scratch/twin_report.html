Rainfall Digital Twin — how every number in it was computed, and where the data came from

An interactive 3D visualization of real rainfall physics over a real property, built end to end from sourced, verifiable data — not synthetic assumptions.

Site 17801 Champagne Dr, Winter Garden FL Coords 28.521732, −81.657073 Live at localhost:8080/twin_viewer.html
01

Purpose

Why this exists, and what question it answers.

This started as a direct question from a CFX team meeting: given that this project's own earlier attempt at a learned GPU surrogate (a graph neural network) turned out to run ~56× slower than the real physics it was meant to accelerate, and hit its own memory ceiling at full scale — can the real physics itself just run fast enough on a GPU to be interactively useful, without needing a trained approximation at all?

This demo answers that at parcel scale: yes. The real shallow-water equations, run as tensor operations on a GPU, solve a full rainfall event in seconds and can be scrubbed through interactively, frame by frame, in a browser.

The second, equally important goal was end-to-end honesty: every input — the terrain, the rainfall intensity, the physics method — is a real, sourced, independently-checkable number, not a placeholder chosen for a nice-looking demo. Section 3 documents exactly where each one came from, including one number that was originally just picked by hand and was corrected once that was caught.

The longer-term purpose is a reusable pattern: the same pipeline (real LiDAR in, real observed rainfall in, real solved physics out) can run on any other CFX corridor parcel or Dix.Hite landscape-design site to visualize where water pools versus sheds, ahead of erosion or stormwater design decisions.

Site and domain

What area is actually being simulated, and how big the house is within it.

625 m²
simulated domain — a 25×25m box centered on the property
~66 m²
the house's own roof footprint — about 10.5% of the domain, the rest is yard/ground
0.10 m
grid cell size the physics is solved at (250×250 cells)

The domain is not the size of the roof — it's a 25×25m box around the whole property, so the yard's slope and low points are included, not just the structure. The roof itself was measured directly from real LiDAR building-return points inside this exact box (1,170 classified building points out of 23,725 total, at a measured local density of 38 points/m²) and separately cross-checked against the elevation mask used to color the 3D model — both land in the same range, a real single-story house footprint, not an artifact of the simulation box size.

What "rasterized to 0.1m cells" means

The real LiDAR point cloud isn't a grid — it's a scattered cloud of individually surveyed 3D points (roughly 40 of them per square meter here). To run a grid-based physics solver, that cloud is rasterized: the domain is divided into a 250×250 grid of 10cm × 10cm cells, and each cell's elevation is set to the average height of the real LiDAR points that fall inside it. This is a resampling step, not synthetic terrain generation — every value in the grid still traces back to real, surveyed points; rasterizing just puts them on a regular grid a numerical solver can operate on.

03

Data sources

Every input, and exactly where it's from.

  • Terrain2018 USGS 3DEP LiDAR (QL2 acquisition), tile USGS_LPC_FL_Peninsular_FDEM_2018_D19_DRRA_LID2019_258656_E.laz — public federal survey data, ~40 points/m² measured density at this site. No synthetic or interpolated terrain anywhere in the model.
  • Rainfall intensity143.5 mm in one hour — a real observed extreme, not an assumed value. Source: GSDR (Global Sub-Daily Rainfall) station US_086638, 31.77 km from this site (the nearest station with usable long-record data — none exist closer), all-time 1-hour maximum, observed during a real storm in 1960. Independently cross-checked against NOAA Atlas 14's statistical rainfall-frequency curve for this exact coordinate: 143.5 mm/hr falls between the curve's 200-year (133 mm/hr) and 500-year (152 mm/hr) 1-hour events — a genuine, severe extreme, consistent with an independent statistical model, not an outlier or a typo.
  • Physics methodLocal-inertial shallow-water equations (Bates et al. 2010 — the same numerical family used by the industry-standard LISFLOOD-FP), reimplemented as vectorized PyTorch tensor operations and run on Apple Silicon GPU (MPS). Manning roughness coefficient 0.030 (mixed suburban), CFL-adaptive timestep, Froude-limited flux for stability on steep roof edges.

A correction made along the way

An earlier version of this rainfall figure was 90 mm/hr, hand-picked with no citation ("near real peak Ian" was the only note attached). When asked directly to justify it, the real GSDR/Atlas 14 data above — already on disk in this project — was checked instead, and the number was corrected to the real observed 143.5 mm/hr figure. A separate, unrelated constant elsewhere in the codebase was also found mislabeled "peak Hurricane Ian" at 77.2 mm/hr when it was actually an unrelated 2023 storm; the real Ian peak (66.8 mm/hr, 2022-09-29 06:00 UTC) was substituted. Both corrections are logged in this project's own change history for a full audit trail.

04

How the water volume was computed

The actual formula, with the real numbers plugged in.

rain rate = 143.5 mm/hr ÷ 3,600,000 (mm/hr → m/s) = 3.986 × 10⁻⁵ m/s volume in = rate × domain area × duration = 3.986×10⁻⁵ m/s × 625 m² × 180 s = 4.484 m³ (4,484 liters) total rain over the event

That figure is a plain closed-form calculation — but the solver itself independently tracks the same quantity as it runs, step by step, and reports where every liter actually went. The two agree to within rounding, which is itself a check that the physics isn't silently creating or losing water:

Compute and memory used

Measured directly, not estimated.

4.5 s
wall-clock time to solve the full 180-second event (3,037 solver steps) on GPU
~4.5 GB
peak RAM — dominated by loading the raw 293.8 MB LiDAR tile, not the solve itself
156M
cells this same GPU solver scales to before hitting a hardware memory ceiling

The 250×250-cell (62,500 cell) grid used for this demo is tiny by the solver's own tested ceiling — a separate resolution-sweep benchmark on this same hardware scaled the identical solver up to 156 million cells before exhausting GPU memory (out-of-memory at 278 million cells, requesting 19.46 GiB). The actual solve for this one-house demo needs only a few megabytes of GPU memory; the ~4.5 GB measured peak is almost entirely the cost of loading and decompressing the raw LiDAR point-cloud tile into memory before cropping it down to this 25×25m box.

06

From solved physics to the visualization

What's actually driving what you see on screen.

The solver runs the full 180-second event once, saving 41 snapshots of the complete depth and velocity fields (a synthetic all-dry t=0 frame, plus 40 real solved frames from t=4.5s through t=180s). Each snapshot is downsampled to a compact grid — 112×112 for depth, a coarser 36×36 for velocity — and packed into a single ~841 KB data file the browser loads once.

Two things in the viewer come directly from that real solved data, not from any separate animation logic:

  • Terrain colorA shader reads the real depth value at each point on the terrain for whichever timestep is selected and colors it from dry through to deep blue — exactly the values the solver computed, nothing smoothed or faked in between.
  • The falling/flowing particles600 particles each run through one real physical journey: fall from the sky, land on the terrain at its real local height, then get pushed by the actual solved velocity field for as long as their cell stays wet, before drying out or leaving the domain and re-entering the sky. A seeded random-number generator makes this deterministic, so dragging the timeline to any moment always reconstructs the same, correct particle state for that exact time — not whatever was left over from wherever the view happened to be before.

The "particle density" slider is cosmetic

It controls how many of the 600 particles are drawn — a visual density control only. It does not change the rainfall rate, the domain size, or the water volume — those are fixed by the real data above and baked into the solved physics before the viewer ever loads.

07

Known limitations

Stated plainly, not glossed over.

  • PhysicsNo soil infiltration or absorption is modeled — every drop of rain either pools on the surface or drains off an edge. This is a real, deliberate simplification for this GPU-physics demo (confirmed by direct code search), and it's conservative: it shows more standing water than a real storm with absorbing soil would produce, not less.
  • Rainfall patternThe rain is held at a constant, flat 143.5 mm/hr for the full 180 seconds. A real storm ramps up and recedes over time — this project already has a real historical hyetograph for a comparable event on disk that a future version could use instead of a flat rate.
  • Data distanceThe nearest usable rain-gauge record is 31.77 km from the site — a real, disclosed distance, not hidden. No closer station has a usable long-enough record.
  • VisualizationThe roof/ground color split on the 3D terrain uses a simple elevation-threshold heuristic, not a surveyed building footprint — it's for legible visualization, not a precise cadastral boundary.
